# Desire Ledger

## How to use scores

Scores run from 0–100 and represent willingness to sacrifice lower-ranked desires when two desires directly conflict. They are **scene-writing weights, not fixed psychology**. Context can temporarily modify a score by up to 15 points. A bodily threat, fresh betrayal, public humiliation, or exceptional tenderness should be named when it changes the ranking.

When two high desires conflict within one character, do not resolve the clash quickly. That is where scenes live.

## High-value collisions

1. **Eve wants him close (91) while wanting to be freely chosen (100).** The only way to satisfy the higher desire is to endanger the lower.
2. **Vincent wants authentic affection (98) and collective trust (92).** Secrecy protects intimacy but makes him look like a collaborator.
3. **Eve wants city stability (94) and abolition (eventually 95).** Until her growth, emergency logic repeatedly postpones justice.
4. **Vincent wants Eve alive (95) and ownership ended (99).** The cleanest revolution may kill her or strip the measure that sustains her.
5. **Eve craves experimentation (96) while fearing emotional powerlessness (90).** Vincent can use bounded private experiments to make surrender feel survivable.
6. **Vincent wants not to manipulate (89) while needing institutional leverage (93).** The most effective leverage is often Eve's desire for him.

## Sacrifice test

A major beat should answer: **Which lower-scored desire does a character sacrifice, and does the sacrifice actually prove the higher-ranked desire?**

Examples:

- Eve spends political capital (influence 81) to protect Vincent (99): meaningful but easy.
- Eve creates an irrevocable legal exit for Vincent (closeness 91) to make free choice possible (100): transformational.
- Vincent refuses to exploit a vulnerable Eve (leverage 93) to preserve non-ownership (89, temporarily +15): proof that his ethics can outrank strategy.
