# Premise

## One-sentence pitch

In a divine city whose economy is stabilized by enslaved mortal champions fighting beneath a living labyrinth, the goddess who owns its most beloved fighter becomes privately dependent on the affection he can withhold—and together they discover that the empire's greatest punishment is also the mechanism keeping the gods alive.

## The setting

The Ninefold City is a mythic Mediterranean power rather than historical Rome. Bronze, linen, bathhouses, sacred courts, patron households, public games, debt bondage, and military pageantry give it a classical silhouette. Gods walk openly among mortals and organize themselves into nine immortal Houses. Their society insists that mortals are lesser beings carrying stolen divinity.

Every House purchases, breeds, trains, or sponsors mortal **votaries**. The official word means "one given to a god"; the honest word is slave. Votaries enter the labyrinth to kill its beasts and retrieve ichor-crystal called **measure**. Some fights occur in grand amphitheaters when the labyrinth opens into public chambers. Others happen below the city where nobody applauds.

The games serve four functions at once:

1. They harvest the resource that maintains divine bodies and the city's protective wards.
2. They punish humanity for the ancestral theft of a god's heart.
3. They turn mortal resistance into entertainment and thereby political legitimacy.
4. They let the Houses speculate on champions, future harvests, and one another's debt.

Vincent is Eve's champion: exceptionally lethal in the labyrinth, unexpectedly disciplined outside it, and popular enough that the crowd chants his name in districts where hers is spoken only in prayer. Eve is an old goddess of thresholds, appetite, and chosen bonds. She can order his body into an arena. She cannot order him to look at her with tenderness.

That asymmetry becomes their private axis.

## Tone promise

- **Dark by context:** ownership, spectacle, exploitation, political violence, and genuine moral compromise.
- **Sweet in the raw relationship:** attentive observation, earned trust, private humor, acts of care, and two adults becoming more honest than their positions permit.
- **Erotically charged by negotiated reversals:** the public owner may privately surrender control; the public captive may choose service that means something precisely because refusal remains possible inside their rituals.
- **Clever rather than merely cruel:** survival comes from reading incentives, delays, bottlenecks, coalitions, and unintended consequences.

## The central dramatic question

Can Eve create conditions in which Vincent can freely choose her without collapsing the divine order that gives her power—and can Vincent love her without turning her desire into merely another resource to exploit?

The romance is complete only when both can answer yes at a cost. A declaration alone cannot solve a structural contradiction.

## Systemic power balance

| Eve controls | Vincent controls |
|---|---|
| legal personhood and punishment | the authenticity of his affection |
| wealth, rooms, guards, and schedules | rare labyrinth competence |
| divine force and longevity | crowd loyalty and mortal credibility |
| official narrative | firsthand knowledge of the oppressed |
| access to comfort and protection | the ability to embarrass, refuse, or emotionally starve her |
| entry into the labyrinth | the systems-sense that may reveal how to end it |

Neither column makes the other harmless. The imbalance is real; the counter-power makes it dramatically unstable.

## Canon guardrails

- Eve and Vincent are adults.
- The story never pretends legal ownership is consent.
- Private submission is valuable only when it is chosen and distinguishable from public coercion.
- Vincent may strategically use Eve's desire, but he must sometimes refuse to do so or risk becoming only a more elegant captor.
- Eve's affection does not absolve her participation in the institution. Repair requires costly action.
- Monsters, politics, and economics return pressure to the romance; they do not replace it.
